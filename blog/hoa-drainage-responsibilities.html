HOA Drainage Responsibilities: What Boards and Property Managers Must Know

June 22, 20266 min read

Few issues land on an HOA board’s agenda more reliably than water. A homeowner’s backyard floods every time it rains and they want to know whose problem it is. A retention pond overflows into a common area. A shared swale erodes until it threatens a row of patios. Sorting out HOA drainage is rarely about the water alone — it is about who owns the problem, who pays to fix it, and what the association is legally on the hook for when things go wrong. Boards and the property managers who advise them need a clear grasp of all three.

The challenge is that responsibility is often genuinely murky. Governing documents were drafted years ago, plat maps show drainage easements nobody remembers granting, and the line between a homeowner’s lot and association-maintained common area blurs exactly where water tends to collect. This guide lays out how boards and managers should think about HOA drainage obligations — and how to solve shared problems permanently once responsibility is established.

Who Actually Owns the Drainage Problem?

The first question in any HOA water dispute is jurisdictional. Most associations are responsible for common areas, shared infrastructure, and the drainage easements recorded on the plat, while individual owners are responsible for grading and water management within their own lots. The complication is that water does not respect those boundaries, and the documents that define them are often vague.

  • Common-area systems: Retention ponds, shared swales, culverts, and the drainage easements crossing multiple lots almost always fall to the association.
  • Lot-level grading: Water that ponds entirely within one homeowner’s yard, with no easement involved, is typically the owner’s responsibility.
  • Gray-zone runoff: Water that originates on common area and damages a lot — or vice versa — is where disputes erupt and where boards need documentation.
  • Recorded easements: Drainage easements grant the association both the right and, often, the duty to maintain the flow path, even when it crosses private property.

Before any board commits funds, it should read the CC&Rs carefully, pull the recorded plat, and ideally have legal counsel confirm the boundaries of responsibility. The Liability Boards Underestimate

An association that knows about a drainage defect in a common area and fails to act is exposing itself to real liability. Standing water on shared walkways is a slip-and-fall hazard. Uncontrolled runoff that damages a member’s home invites claims of negligence. And erosion that undermines shared infrastructure can escalate from a maintenance item into a structural emergency. The cost of inaction compounds quietly until a single storm forces an expensive, reactive fix.

Boards that manage parking, clubhouses, or retail-adjacent common areas also carry stormwater compliance duties they may not realize, a topic we cover in parking lot stormwater compliance. Understanding those obligations early keeps the association ahead of both regulators and litigation.

An HOA board’s biggest drainage mistake is treating a known common-area defect as next year’s budget item. Water does not wait for the reserve study, and neither does liability.

Why HOA Drainage Repairs Keep Coming Back

When associations do act, they often choose the cheapest available fix: regrading a swale, dropping in a catch basin, or installing a gravel-and-pipe French drain across a wet common area. These solutions feel decisive but tend to fail on the same timeline. Swales silt in and re-erode. French drains clog with sediment and roots within a few years. And because much of the Southeast sits on flat, heavy clay, gravity-dependent systems struggle to move water at all once the modest slope they relied on settles out.

The result is a recurring line item that frustrates owners and erodes trust in the board. Each repair buys a few seasons before the same wet spot returns, and the association ends up paying for the same problem several times over. How Boards Should Approach a Drainage Project

A well-run HOA drainage project follows a clear sequence. First, document the problem — photograph standing water during and after storms and confirm whether the affected area is common or private. Second, establish responsibility against the CC&Rs and plat. Third, get a professional assessment that identifies the actual subsurface flow path, not just the visible ponding. Only then should the board solicit solutions, prioritizing permanence over the lowest bid.

Associations should also keep federal guidance in view; the EPA Soak Up the Rain program and the EPA construction and commercial stormwater rules offer a useful framework for managing runoff responsibly.
