Drainage Red Flags to Check Before You Buy a Home in the Southeast

September 12, 20267 min read

A drainage problem is invisible on the day you tour a house. The sun is out, the yard looks fine, the basement smells like fresh paint. Three months and one heavy Georgia storm later, you learn what the previous owner already knew. Water problems are among the most expensive surprises a Southeast homebuyer inherits — and they are almost never called out in a standard home inspection.

Why drainage rarely shows up in a standard inspection

A general home inspector checks that the sump pump turns on and that gutters are attached. They are not there during a rain event, they do not test soil percolation, and they are not evaluating whether the lot can actually move water away from the structure. Drainage is a wet-weather, whole-site problem, and most inspections are a dry-day, inside-the-house exercise. That gap is where buyers get hurt.

The good news: the house almost always tells you the truth if you know what to look at. Here are the seven red flags worth a careful look on your walk-through.

The Seven Red Flags

1. Water stains or efflorescence on foundation walls

In the basement or crawl space, look low on the walls for horizontal staining, a chalky white mineral crust (efflorescence), or a distinct water line. These are the fingerprints of water that has sat against the foundation. Fresh paint on only the lower portion of a basement wall is a tell, not a comfort.

2. Grading that slopes toward the house

Stand at each corner and look at the ground. Soil should fall away from the foundation — roughly six inches over the first ten feet. On flat lots and settled red-clay sites across the Southeast, the grade often runs the wrong way, funneling roof and yard water straight to the wall it should be leaving.

3. Downspouts that end at the foundation

A roof sheds thousands of gallons in a single storm. If downspouts dump right at the wall, into a splash block, or into a buried pipe that surfaces two feet away, that concentrated volume is being delivered exactly where you least want it. Disconnected or crushed underground extensions are common and easy to miss.

4. An existing French drain, sump pump, or drywell

Existing drainage is not reassurance — it is evidence of a known, prior water problem. The real questions are how old the system is, whether it still works, and whether it solved the cause or just chased the symptom. A French drain in clay soil has a limited lifespan; a sump pump running on a dry week is telling you something.

5. Fresh mulch, sod, or landscaping in odd places

A thick new bed of mulch or a fresh strip of sod along one side of the house can be exactly what it looks like — or it can be covering a chronic low spot that ponds after every rain. Be curious about brand-new landscaping that hugs the foundation on just one elevation.

6. Sticking doors, stair-step cracks, and uneven floors

Chronic soil moisture makes clay swell and shrink, and that movement shows up as diagonal cracks in brick or drywall, doors and windows that stick, and floors that feel off. Not every crack is drainage, but moisture-driven foundation movement is one of the costliest outcomes of a water problem left alone.

7. The lot next door — and uphill

Water does not respect property lines. A neighbor whose lot sits higher, or whose downspouts and grading push runoff toward the home you are buying, becomes your problem the day you close. Walk the property line and note where water would travel in a storm.

How to check it in one visit

  • Go outside and walk all four corners — note which way the ground slopes at each
  • Trace every downspout to where it actually discharges
  • Get into the basement or crawl space with a flashlight and look low on the walls
  • Ask the seller's disclosure directly: any prior water intrusion, and any drainage work done?
  • If it rained recently, look for the last places still wet or muddy
  • Note any higher neighboring lot that drains toward the property

What a red flag actually means for the deal

One red flag is a question, not a deal-breaker. Several together mean you should price the fix into your offer before you sign, not after you move in. A true drainage solution is not a bigger gutter or a load of topsoil — on the flat, clay-heavy lots common here, gravity systems keep failing because they need slope the site does not have.
